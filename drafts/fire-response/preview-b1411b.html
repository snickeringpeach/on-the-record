Who Waits Longest for the Fire Department

A Providence ambulance or engine reaches a medical call in a median 6 minutes. In Ward 2 it takes 9, and more than half the calls wait longer than eight. The wait follows how far the nearest firehouse is, not how much the neighborhood earns.

By Liam Freaney · from the Providence Fire Department’s own incident reports in the federal fire-incident data

The record

The Fire Department files a report for every incident with the National Fire Incident Reporting System. In the five years it reported in full (2019, 2021, 2022, 2023 and 2025) it filed 135,539 incidents with both an alarm time and an arrival time; 90,814 were medical or rescue calls. The wait here is the time from the alarm to the first unit on scene, of any kind.

Citywide, a medical call waits a median 6 minutes; one in ten waits 10 or more, and 18% wait more than eight. Fires: a median 5 minutes, one in ten 8 or more (2,047 fires).

NFIRS public data release, Providence (FDID 02800), 2019–2025; 2020 and 2024 are partial years and left out. Waits kept between 0 and 60 minutes; mutual aid given to other towns dropped. Each incident placed at its address on the 2025 tax roll (57%) or the middle of its street. Eight minutes is a reading line chosen here, not a City or national standard. (B1411)

Across the 15 wards, the average wait and the distance to the nearest engine house rank together (rank correlation 0.80; across census tracts, 0.58). Against tract income the relation is weak and runs the other way from the usual story: the richest fifth of tracts waits a median 7 minutes, the poorest fifth 6 (rank correlation +0.19). MODEL

Ward 2 is slowest by a distance, and farthest from a firehouse. About 1,980 of its 3,696 medical calls go to three addresses at the north end of Blackstone Boulevard, one the tax roll codes as a psychiatric facility and two as 24-hour care homes. [C] The question for the City is how the far end of Blackstone Boulevard is covered, and whether those runs are emergencies or scheduled transfers; the federal record doesn’t say.

Before and after the 2017 closures

On January 8, 2017 the City closed two East Side engine houses, 280 Rochambeau Avenue and 155 Humboldt Avenue, under the new firefighters’ contract. The area those two houses were nearest to waited a mean 7.3 minutes on a medical call before (24% over eight) and 7.7 after (33%). The rest of the city went the other way: 7.0 to 6.4 minutes, 19% to 16%.

Against the rest of the city, the closed houses’ area lost about a minute (+0.96 minutes on the mean; 95% bootstrap 0.73–1.16) and 12 points on the share over eight minutes. MODEL Ward 2 went from 8.1 to 9.3 minutes, and from 37% to 55% over eight.

Two things make that gap. The rest of Providence got faster and the north East Side did not. And the slowest addresses grew: the three Blackstone Boulevard institutions went from 16% to 21% of the area’s medical calls. Leave those three addresses out and the area is flat, 6.8 minutes before and 6.8 after, while the city improved: a relative loss of 0.5 minutes and 6 points.

[C] The fair reading: since the 2017 closures, the north East Side has not shared the citywide gain in response, and the calls that wait longest, to the three institutions on Blackstone Boulevard, are the ones the closed houses were closest to. Not that the closures slowed response by a set number of minutes: the before years are thin, and changes in dispatch or reporting between the two eras can’t be separated from the closures here.
