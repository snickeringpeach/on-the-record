Who Pays for the Next Flood

[C] In 2010, Rhode Island rebuilt from the March floods more on loans than on flood insurance. Since then, the places that flooded have dropped a third of their flood policies.

By Liam Freaney · from FEMA’s and the Small Business Administration’s public disaster files

What 2010 cost, and who paid

In March and April 2010, rivers in Kent and Providence counties flooded thousands of homes. The federal disaster declaration was DR-1894. Here is who paid for the rebuilding, by program:

ProgramPaid

FEMA aid to towns and the state (Public Assistance)$26.0M
Flood insurance (NFIP), 1,747 claims$43.5M
SBA disaster loans, homes and businesses$46.8M

OpenFEMA Individuals and Households Program and Public Assistance funded-projects summaries, DR-1894; OpenFEMA NFIP redacted claims, losses dated March 12 to April 12, 2010; SBA Disaster Loan Data, FY2010 workbook, FEMA 1894 rows. Pulled Oct. 18, 2026. then_and_now.json.

The largest single source was loans. People borrowed more to rebuild than flood insurance paid out. In Kent County, homeowners and businesses borrowed $18.1M against $14.7M from flood insurance. In Warwick, half the 2010 recovery money was debt. In Coventry, three quarters.

Since then, FEMA has obligated $1.05M in hazard mitigation in the Kent County towns. It went to 4 buildings.

Loans as a share of 2010 recovery: SBA approved loans over SBA loans plus NFIP payments, by town, sba_1894_by_town.csv. Mitigation: OpenFEMA Hazard Mitigation Assistance subapplications v2, Rhode Island, 2010–2026, hma_ri_towns.csv and hma_ri_projects.csv.

[C] The buildings that flooded in 2010 were mostly rebuilt where they stood, on borrowed money.

It happened again, in the same places

In December 2023 and January 2024, two more federal declarations followed (DR-4765, DR-4766). They were smaller: 205 flood insurance claims, against 1,747 in 2010. They landed in the same towns: Warwick, West Warwick, Cranston, Johnston.

They landed in the same neighborhoods too. The public claims file has no addresses, but it does give each claim’s census block group. In the Kent towns, 77 of the 85 claims in 2023–24 were in block groups that also claimed in 2010. Every one was in a census tract that claimed in 2010.

OpenFEMA NFIP redacted claims, Rhode Island, by census block group. Every block group, both periods: bg_2010_vs_2023_24.csv; totals: summary_bg.json; by town: then_and_now_towns.csv.

Not yet on the record

  • Whether the 2023–24 claims hit the same buildings as 2010. The Rhode Island Emergency Management Agency keeps the list of properties that flood again and again. I asked for it on Oct. 18, 2026.

Fewer of them are insured now

In 2010, Rhode Island had 16,217 flood insurance policies. In 2024 it had 11,791, down 27%. In the areas that flooded in 2010 the drop is about 30%. Warwick lost 47% of its policies, Cranston 50%.

OpenFEMA NFIP redacted policies (v3), policies in force by effective year. By town and year: takeup_town_year.csv; totals: takeup.json.

Premiums alone don’t explain it. Policies kept falling after 2018 even where the typical premium went down. [C] The public file can’t say why people dropped coverage: whether a mortgage was paid off, the bill went up, or something else. It shows when.

Policies and median cost in 2025 dollars, by year, mandatory or voluntary purchase, and flood zone: why_fell.csv.

It shows up in the 2023–24 storms. This time FEMA grants to households ($19.4M) were nearly double what flood insurance paid ($10.2M). In 2010 it was the other way around.

Providence: the basements

Providence flooded too, but not the way Kent County did. Three storms brought federal declarations between September 2023 and January 2024 (DR-4753, DR-4765, DR-4766). In the city, 578 households registered with FEMA. FEMA recorded flood damage at 143 of them, and for 121 of those the water was in the basement.

The flooded basements were not by the rivers. Block groups whose center sits 100 feet or more above sea level had 3.2 flood-damaged registrants per 1,000 households; those under 20 feet had 0.2. [C] Water in a basement on a hill came up through the floor drain or the foundation: the sewer backing up, or groundwater.

Eighteen of the city’s 153 block groups flooded in two or three of the storms. Those eighteen hold 81 of the 143 flood-damaged households.

OpenFEMA Individuals and Households Program valid registrations (v2), Providence tracts 0001–0037, by 2020 census block group; USGS 3DEP elevation at each block group’s internal point. pvd_bg.csv, pvd_summary.json. (B951)

Almost none of it was insured

Of the 121 households with a flooded basement, 3 had flood insurance. 79% of the flooded households in the eighteen block groups had homeowners insurance, which doesn’t cover flood; 1% had flood insurance.

Flood insurance did pay out in Providence on those storm dates: 12 claims, $2.24M. 8 of the 12 were non-residential buildings, and 9 were in the high-risk river zone (AE). Citywide, 3 claims involved a basement. The 2 that paid, $112,000 between them, were both non-residential buildings. The one house with a basement claim was paid nothing. In the eighteen block groups, 4 claims paid $1.06M, all in the AE river zone, and none for a basement. Sixteen of the eighteen had no claim at all.

[C] The flood insurance money went mostly to commercial and larger residential buildings on the rivers. The basements on the hills were paid for by the people who owned them.

The same block groups flooded in 2010

In March 2010 (DR-1894), about 538 Providence households registered flood damage with FEMA, 94% of them in the basement. 16 of the eighteen block groups were hit then too. They took 45% of the city’s 2010 flood registrations while holding 15% of its households: 21.8 per 1,000 households, against 4.9 in the rest of the city. Four block groups flooded in all four storms: two in Elmhurst, one in Washington Park, one in Silver Lake.

OpenFEMA IHP valid registrations (v2), DR-1894, Providence County, 2010 block groups placed on 2020 block groups by land-area share (Census Bureau 2020–2010 block group relationship file), so 2010 counts are allocations. b962.json. (B962)

Same homes, or same streets?

FEMA’s public file can’t say. Its finest location is the block group: no address, no street, nothing that follows a household from one disaster to the next. What it can say is a limit. At most 59 of the 81 flood-damaged households of 2023–24 in the eighteen could have been 2010 registrants in the same block group. At least 22 were not.

Leave out one block group in Wanskuck, where 12 apartments flooded on the first floor in September 2023, and [C] the rest look like 2010: owner-occupied houses with water in the basement, in the same block groups. The water was shallower this time. 26% of 2010’s flooded households in the eighteen had six inches or more; 11% in 2023–24.

[C] FEMA’s damage dollars don’t compare across the two floods. They are inspection schedule values, and the schedule changed.

OpenFEMA IHP valid registrations (v2) field list (100 fields); the 2010 and 2023–24 registrants in the eighteen, side by side. b962a.json, b962a_compare.csv. (B962a)

Renters

Renters are 46% of the households in the eighteen block groups and 2 of the 64 households FEMA recorded with a flooded basement there. [C] That is more likely a gap in who registers, and in how a shared basement gets recorded, than a sign renters were spared.

A renter’s own flood cover is a tenant (contents) policy. On the day of the January 2024 storm, the eighteen block groups held 2 of them, for 5,096 renter households. On July 1, 2026, they held 1. None of the 19 renters FEMA recorded with flood damage in the city had flood insurance.

Census Bureau ACS 2020–24 5-year, table B25003, by block group; OpenFEMA NFIP redacted policies (v3), tenant policies in force on each storm day and July 1, 2026. b1034.json, renters_bg.csv. (B1034)

Not yet on the record (Providence)

  • Which flooded streets are on the combined sewer. The Narragansett Bay Commission has the map; no public copy turned up.
  • Whether the same houses flooded in 2010 and 2023–24. That needs addresses, which FEMA and the city hold and don’t publish.
