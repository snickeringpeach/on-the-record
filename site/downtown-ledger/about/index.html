How this record is made

Every row in the ledger is on it because a peer downtown has the thing or the city itself has proposed it, never because the author thinks a downtown ought to. Every judgment about a walk is one person's estimate of what a typical pedestrian would do, recorded the same way each time, with a reason on every no.

The ledger

The ledger asks three things in order: what downtown has, what it does not, and how long the missing take to arrive.

A row is a kind of thing a downtown has. A row is on the list for one of two reasons only: a peer downtown has the thing inside its own declared boundary, or the city itself has proposed it. Nothing is on the list because the author thinks a downtown ought to have it.

Rows come in three kinds. An amenity (a grocery, a pharmacy, a cinema) is there, partial, missing or not yet called. A count (residents, jobs, housing units, vacancy) is a number with a year and a source, shown beside the peers’ numbers. A project (the bus hub, the station renovation) is something the city has proposed, with its stage, what it needs first and how many years out it is.

The line

Each city is measured on one line, drawn by that city: Worcester’s business improvement district and New Haven’s Town Green district. For Providence the true counterpart is the Downtown Improvement District, whose map is not published. Until it is in hand, the Downcity zoning overlay (107 acres) stands in, and every page says so. Every status and every count is taken on that one line. A figure that exists only on another boundary is shown as context, never as the row’s number.

Distance to the line is measured straight from the place, and falls in one of four bands: inside; at the edge, within 150 metres (across a boundary street or one short block out); near, within 800 metres (about a ten-minute walk); and outside. An amenity is there when one is open to the public inside the line. It is partial when none is inside but one is at the edge, or when a stated test fails (a grocery must sell fresh produce and meat, not only prepared or specialty food). It is missing otherwise, and the row names the nearest one and how far it is. A blank is shown as a blank.

The ledger’s own calls on which places belong downtown (the mall, the State House, the station, the canal) are kept in a column of their own, Providence by the ledger’s call. The peers have no such calls, so that column never enters the comparison.

Freshness

An amenity whose newest evidence is more than twelve months old is marked stale, with the date of that evidence. Counts carry their data year and are replaced when newer data comes out.

Street View pictures are shown with the date Google took them, for orientation only. Nothing on the ledger is read off them.

For the missing rows, years out is how long the thing takes to grow. Peer-derived rows are years out because demand is missing; proposed rows are years out because of what has to happen first, and each of those is listed with a source. A dependency without a source is not shown.

The walks

Whether a place is inside downtown, adjacent to it, or outside is declared, with a one-line reason, not computed. Each place is indexed to a mode: a destination for a driver is not the same thing as a destination on foot.

A walk is a judgment about one link between two places: would a typical pedestrian make it. Not the recorder, who would walk almost anything; the average person standing there. Recorded twice where it splits: once for someone arriving in the city, once for someone who lives here. Every no and every maybe carries a reason in plain words, and the reason is the checkable part.

Most bad walks are refused before the first step, so the main observation is a sightline taken at the origin, facing the route. Block-face counts are taken only on routes where the sightline says go and people still do not.
