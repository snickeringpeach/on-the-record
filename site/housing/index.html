Providence Permitted 778 New Homes in 2025, or 324, Depending on Which Government Count You Read

The State has set Providence a goal of 3,010 new homes over the next five years. The State’s housing report and the Census Bureau disagree on how many the City permits now, by more than two to one.

By Liam Freaney · from the Census Bureau, the RI Executive Office of Housing and the City’s own records

The goal

Under Housing 2030, the State’s housing plan, each city and town is expected to permit new homes equal to a share of its housing each year: 0.4 percent for towns without many jobs or much transit, 0.6 percent for towns with more jobs, and 0.8 percent for places with both. Providence is in the third group. Its goal is 3,010 homes from 2026 through 2030, about 602 a year, of which 301 are to be affordable.

RI Department of Housing, Housing 2030 municipal growth categories and production goals (housing.ri.gov/media/3401). Providence’s goal is 0.8 percent of 75,257 homes, times five years.

Two counts of the same permits

Two government surveys count the building permits each city issues for new homes. The Census Bureau’s Building Permits Survey takes what each city reports, month by month. The State’s Department of Housing, renamed the Executive Office of Housing in 2025, surveys each city once a year, and its report says it uses that survey because it is “more accurate than the Census survey.” For Providence in 2025, the Census count is 324 homes and the State count is 778. Measured against the goal’s yearly pace, which starts in 2026, the first is 54% of it and the second 129%. In 2024 the two counts were 269 and 622.

The gap runs statewide. The Census count for all 39 cities and towns in 2025 is 1,738 homes; the State’s is 3,778. In 5 towns the Census figure is partly the Bureau’s own estimate, because the town did not report every month: Coventry, Foster, Johnston, Little Compton and Newport. The State’s 2025 report marks 5 municipalities as not having completed its survey before publication, Coventry, Johnston, North Providence, North Smithfield and Warren, and still prints permit and occupancy figures for each of them without saying where those came from. The Housing 2030 goals are written in permits, and the plan does not say which survey will be used to measure them.

In 5 of the 39 the two counts match exactly. The widest gaps are in Providence, 454 homes, and Warwick, 297.

The gap is apartments

Split by building size, most of the 2025 difference is in buildings of two or more homes. Across the 39 cities and towns the State’s survey has 2,698 homes permitted in such buildings and the Census count has 1,008, a difference of 1,690. For single-family houses the two are 1,080 and 730, a difference of 350. In 5 towns the State reports 20 or more homes in multifamily buildings and the Census reports none: Bristol, Newport, North Kingstown, North Smithfield and Pawtucket.

It was not always so. Pawtucket’s two counts were never more than 11 homes apart in any year from 2018 through 2023. In 2024 the State recorded 152 homes in its multifamily buildings and the Census recorded 2; in 2025 the figures were 147 and 0. Middletown’s counts matched exactly every year from 2018 through 2023, then split by 114 homes in 2024.

The two surveys do not say why. The Census count is built from what each city reports; the State’s comes from its own annual survey of each city. Records requests to Pawtucket for its reports to both, filed Oct. 2, 2026, are meant to show what the city sent each of them.

How the State counts what got built

A permit is not a home. For 2025 the State counts homes finished by asking each city and town how many certificates of occupancy it issued, the document that lets people move in. Providence reported 727: 60 single-family houses, 168 homes in two-family buildings, 29 in buildings of three or four, and 470 in buildings of five or more. Statewide the count is 2,162. The report says the State had not tracked this before, “so there is no historical data available for comparison.”

The 2024 report used a formula instead. For single-family houses it took the year before’s permits and multiplied by 0.66, the share of New England single-family permits that become houses within a year in a national Census survey. For apartments it counted certificates of occupancy, but only for developments with affordable units, and said the method “does not capture the full set of multifamily completions in the state.” For Providence in 2024 that gave 33 houses and 169 apartments, 202 in all; the 169 apartments were four affordable developments: Copley Chambers II and III (124), Joseph Caffey Apartments (39), Portland Homes (5) and one unit at 1192 Westminster St. The two years are not comparable.

What Providence told the Census and the State

Both surveys get their numbers from the City. From 2018 through 2025, Providence’s reports to the Census add to 1,362 homes and its answers to the State’s survey add to 4,330. They matched once, in 2022. In 2018 the City reported 1 new home to the Census and 892 to the State; its own published permit records for that year add to 123. In 2023 it reported 5 to the Census, with all 12 months filed, and 1,081 to the State. In 2017, a year it reported 4 homes to the Census, its permit records include a 15-story, 202-apartment building at 169 Canal St.

What got built, parcel by parcel

The Tax Assessor records the year each building went up and how many homes it holds. By that record, 2,559 homes in Providence are in buildings put up from 2010 through 2023, on 819 parcels. That includes 222 the assessor records in student housing; it leaves out 4 hotel parcels. 698 of the homes, 27%, sit on 15 parcels under tax stabilization agreements. In the 14 new buildings with 20 or more homes, not counting student housing, 683 of 1,013 homes, 67%, are on parcels with an agreement.

Asked and answered

  • Which count the State will use to judge the Housing 2030 goals. The Executive Office of Housing, Oct. 5, 2026: “EOH will use housing permit data collected directly from municipalities to measure progress toward the 15,000-unit Housing 2030 goal.”
  • Where the State’s 2025 figures come from for the 5 towns that had not completed its survey. The Executive Office of Housing, Oct. 5, 2026: “Those five municipalities had responded to the permit and certificate of occupancy questions. While they had not completed the other portions of the broader data request by the time of publication, their permit and CO data were included in the statewide totals.”
  • Why the State’s count runs above the Census count. The Executive Office of Housing, Oct. 5, 2026: the Census survey “does not capture conversions and relies on a sampling of municipal survey responses and then imputes data from those survey responses,” and “the difference between the figures reflects, in large part, the different methodologies used to collect the data.” Asked how much of the difference is conversions: “We do not have an estimate for how much might have come from conversions, so I recommend asking Providence for that number.”
